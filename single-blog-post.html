Clarity is a
creative advantage.

A practical note on getting aligned around the problem before deciding what to make.

AKAlify editorial 8 Nov 2026 6 min read Strategy
Start a project All articles

On this page

    Share

    Most projects do not fail because the design was weak. They fail because three people walked into the room with three different ideas of what the work was for — and nobody said the sentence out loud.

    The cost of a fuzzy brief

    A fuzzy brief is not a small thing. It quietly moves cost downstream: into revisions, into rebuilds, and into the week where everyone finally agrees on a direction the work was never pointed at.

    We see the pattern often enough to name it. A team starts with enthusiasm, sketches arrive quickly, and somewhere around the second review the conversation stops being about the customer and starts being about taste. That is not a design problem. It is a decision that was never made.

    Clarity is not extra work you do before the real work. It is the work, and everything after it gets cheaper.

    What clarity actually looks like

    Clarity is rarely a document. It is usually one or two sentences your team can repeat without checking a deck: who this is for, what changes for them, and what we are deliberately not doing.

    When those sentences exist, decisions get faster. A layout choice stops being an argument about preference and becomes a question with an answer — does this help the person we named?

    Three signs it is working

    • People describe the project the same way in different rooms.
    • Feedback sounds like “this is off-brief” instead of “I don’t like blue”.
    • You can say what the first release will not include — and nobody flinches.

    Three questions before any screen

    1. Who is this for, specifically? Not the segment. The person, the moment, and what they are trying to finish.
    2. What should be true after they use it? One outcome you could actually verify a month later.
    3. What are we leaving out? The clearest briefs spend as much space on the “no” list as the “yes”.

    Rule of thumb

    If the brief cannot survive being explained to a new team member in ninety seconds, it is not clear enough yet.

    Making it stick

    Write the sentences down where the work happens — at the top of the project board, on the wall, in the file everyone opens first. A decision nobody can find is a decision that will be remade, badly, in week five.

    Then revisit it once, deliberately. Priorities move. A sentence that was true in January deserves a five-minute review in March rather than a slow, unspoken drift that everyone notices and nobody names.

    Start with the sentence

    Before the moodboard, before the wireframes, before the kickoff deck: write the sentence. If it takes an argument to get there, that argument is cheaper now than it will ever be again.

    That is the whole advantage. Not more ideas — fewer, clearer ones, moving in the same direction.
